The best one-on-one I ever had started with silence. My manager sat down, opened his notebook, and said nothing for a full ten seconds. Then: "What's the thing you're most avoiding right now?"

I didn't have an answer ready. That was the point.

<!--more-->

Most managers walk into a one-on-one with an agenda. Updates, blockers, roadmap check-ins. It's not bad management — it's just incomplete. The meeting gets ticked off the calendar and nothing actually surfaces.

The shift happens when you stop treating one-on-ones as status reports and start treating them as excavations. You're not looking for what's on top. You're looking for what's been quietly accumulating underneath.

## The questions that actually work

Not "how are things going?" — that gets you "fine." Not "any blockers?" — that gets you the sanitised version.

Try these instead:

- *What's the thing you're most avoiding right now?*
- *If you could change one thing about how the team works, what would it be?*
- *What have you learned in the last month that surprised you?*
- *Where do you feel like you're operating below your capability?*

None of these are magic. What they have in common is that they require actual thought. They can't be answered on autopilot, which means the person answering them has to actually show up.

## The silence is the tool

The instinct when someone pauses is to fill the gap. Restate the question, soften it, offer options. Resist this. The pause means they're thinking. Thinking is the whole point.

Silence is uncomfortable for the person asking the question too. Sitting in it is a skill, and it's one worth developing. What comes out of a genuine pause is almost always more valuable than whatever you would have said to fill it.

Good questions are only half the work. The other half is getting out of the way and letting the answer arrive.
