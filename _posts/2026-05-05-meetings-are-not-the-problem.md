Every few months, a certain kind of post makes the rounds. Someone announces they've deleted all their recurring meetings. Productivity soared. Focus returned. They wrote a blog post about it, which you are now reading in a meeting.

I understand the impulse. Meetings can be genuinely terrible — unfocused, overlong, full of people who didn't need to be there, covering things that could have been an email. But the response to bad meetings is not fewer meetings. It's better ones.

<!--more-->

## What meetings actually do

The anti-meeting position tends to treat synchronous communication as inherently wasteful, with async as the virtuous default. This misses something. Async communication is very good at transmitting information. It's considerably worse at building shared understanding, resolving genuine ambiguity, or moving through disagreement.

Some conversations need the back-and-forth. Some decisions need the room. Some relationships need the face time, virtual or otherwise. Strip all of that out in the name of productivity and you often get a team that's efficient at executing and bad at knowing whether it's executing the right things.

## The actual problems

The problems with most meetings are specific and fixable:

No clear purpose. Every meeting should be able to answer "what does done look like for this conversation?" If it can't, it probably shouldn't exist.

The wrong people in the room. Invite the people who need to decide or who have information the decision requires. Everyone else is an audience, and audiences belong in async updates.

No end to the recurring ones. A meeting that was useful six months ago may no longer be. Recurring meetings should have a review cadence, or at minimum a presumption that they can be cancelled when there's nothing to discuss.

## The thing worth protecting

The best meetings I've been in felt like thinking together. Ideas developed in real time, positions shifted, something got decided that couldn't have been decided any other way. That's not inefficiency — that's the actual work.

The goal isn't to have fewer meetings. It's to have meetings worth having.
