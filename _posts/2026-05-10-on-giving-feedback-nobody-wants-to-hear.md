At some point in your career, someone needs to hear something from you that they are not going to enjoy. This is one of the more uncomfortable parts of working with other humans, and most people handle it badly — including, at various points, me.

The two failure modes are mirror images of each other. The first is avoidance: the feedback never gets given, the problem persists, and eventually it surfaces in a worse way. The second is bluntness used as a virtue: the feedback gets delivered with a kind of aggressive directness that prioritises the speaker's discomfort-reduction over the listener's ability to actually receive it.

<!--more-->

## What feedback is actually for

Feedback isn't self-expression. It's not a performance of honesty or a way to demonstrate that you're the kind of person who "tells it like it is." It's a tool for changing something. If the feedback doesn't land in a way that creates change, it didn't work — regardless of how accurate it was.

This reframe matters because it moves the responsibility. If the message isn't received, that's partly on the delivery. The question isn't just "did I say the true thing?" but "did I say it in a way that could actually be heard?"

## The things that actually help

Be specific. "Your communication style is a problem" is not feedback. "In last Tuesday's meeting, you interrupted Sarah three times while she was mid-sentence" is feedback. Specific things can be acted on. Generalisations just generate defensiveness.

Separate observation from interpretation. "You seemed disengaged" is interpretation. "You checked your phone four times in a thirty-minute meeting" is observation. Start with what you saw, not what you concluded from it.

Don't soften it into meaninglessness. The "feedback sandwich" — compliment, criticism, compliment — is so well-understood that people immediately tune out the compliments and wait for the but. Just say the thing, with care, and let it land.

## The part nobody talks about

Hard feedback requires an ongoing relationship to be useful. A piece of difficult feedback from someone you trust lands completely differently than the same words from someone you don't. Which means if you've been avoiding giving feedback, you may also have been avoiding building the kind of relationship where it could actually help. That's worth sitting with.
